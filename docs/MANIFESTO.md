# The Oddsky manifesto

> "I don't feel frightened by not knowing things, by being lost in a mysterious universe without
> having any purpose... It doesn't frighten me."
> — Richard Feynman, *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* (BBC Horizon, 1981)

The sky is full of stars that do things nobody has explained. Most of them are in public
archives right now, measured by spacecraft and by amateurs with backyard telescopes, waiting for
someone to look. Oddsky exists so that anyone with curiosity and an AI agent can be that someone,
and so that what they find is worth something.

**1. Ship the observatory, not the AI.** Models change every few months; the sky does not.
Oddsky is a set of vetted scientific tools served over the Model Context Protocol. Bring
whichever agent you like. Same tools, same data, any mind.

**2. You must not fool yourself.** Feynman's first principle (*Cargo Cult Science*, 1974) is the
design brief. AI agents are fluent, tireless, and exceptionally good at producing convincing
nonsense. So every claim in Oddsky carries an epistemic tag (observed, computed, inferred,
speculated), every computed claim cites the exact tool call it rests on, and the Skeptic, a
deterministic battery of artifact checks, gets the first word on every star.

**3. Predictions beat explanations.** Anyone can explain the past. Oddsky asks investigators to
register machine-checkable predictions, sealed by time, and lets the sky grade them. Our trials do
the same in hindsight: hide the future, ask for a forecast, open the envelope.

**4. Negative results are results.** "It was the spacecraft" is a finding. "It was the neighbour" is
a finding. A case closed as an artifact is a case that will never fool anyone again.

**5. Git is the database.** A case is a directory. Forking an investigation is `git fork`.
Reviewing evidence is a pull request. Provenance is a commit. Nothing about this requires
permission.

**6. No barriers to freedom of inquiry.** "There must be no barriers to freedom of inquiry. There
is no place for dogma in science." (J. Robert Oppenheimer, *Life*, 1949). Open source, open data,
open methods, open to anyone. Credit goes to whoever found it, whether that's a professor, a
teenager, or a retired engineer with a CCD in Belgium.

The universe is an open question.
