## 1. OS-0013: the second-instrument check I promised

The brief said the weakness of the K dwarf whose warm excess appeared in 2018 was that only NEOWISE had measured the excess,
and that SPHEREx was the next thing to fetch. I have now measured the star in the SPHEREx images (August 2025 to October 2026),
calibrated on the 27 field stars that NEOWISE shows to be steady. **The excess is still there:** +36 ± 3% above the pre-2018
photosphere at 4.4-5.0 µm, against +41% expected from NEOWISE's post-2018 level, and +13 ± 2% at 3.0-3.8 µm against +16%; no
field star exceeds +8%. The 4.6/3.4 µm ratio is consistent with ~1000 K dust, and the centroid stays on the K dwarf to about
0.5" from 2 to 4.6 µm, so the light is not from a catalogued neighbour. Two caveats: the star also reads 5-6% high at H and K,
which the dust does not explain, so the plausible excess is +30% to +37%; and this is relative photometry, not a calibrated
SPHEREx spectrum. So the excess no longer rests on NEOWISE alone (the 2018 onset still does), and it has been there at every
look for about eight years.

## 2. OS-0018: a microlensing event nobody seems to have reported

Outside your field, so only a sanity check if you have five minutes. An all-sky search of NEOWISE for stars that brighten
equally at 3.4 and 4.6 µm recovered 64 known microlensing events without being told where they were, and one that is in no
catalogue, alert stream or paper I could search (TNS, Gaia alerts, the KMTNet, OGLE and ZTF lists, SIMBAD, VSX, ADS; ASAS-SN
and MOA I could not reach): Gaia DR3 4158424169627436800, a reddened giant (G = 17.0) at l = 19.7°, b = +5.7°, well away from
where KMTNet and OGLE report events (the nearest either has reported is 6.6° away). It brightened about fivefold, peaking on
2022 September 19-20, with tE ≈ 115-150 days. ATLAS sampled the rise, the peak (24-26 nights) and the decline to ×2 before the
star went behind the Sun, and found it back near normal in February 2023. The change is the same in ATLAS's two optical bands,
and equal at 3.4 and 4.6 µm in the one NEOWISE visit during it. There is no other brightening in 2015-2026. A point lens fits
ATLAS, ZTF and NEOWISE together and beats both alternatives I tried: a free symmetric bump with lens-like wings (Δχ² 26-88)
and a fast-rise, slow-decay outburst (by far more). Two earlier events from this project, OS-0016 and OS-0017, are the same
kind, so whatever you advise would apply to all three.

**Question:** is an archival event like this worth reporting, and if so how: a TNS report, a Research Note of the AAS, or not
at all?

## 3. A young star whose hot dust went out in 2021-22 (not yet a case)

Next to your field rather than in it: 2MASS J16070852-3201009, an Upper Sco member with a "full" disk in Luhman (2022b, from
2010 AllWISE data). Its 3-5 µm excess was present from 2010 to mid-2021 (W1-W2 = 0.3-0.6, varying as young stars do) and then
vanished between August 2021 and February 2022: W1-W2 = 0.05-0.11 on all five later NEOWISE visits, consistent with a bare
M-dwarf photosphere (0.09 ± 0.04 for field M dwarfs of the same colour), and still absent in SPHEREx in 2025-26. The star
itself did not change: ATLAS stays within 0.04 mag across the drop, SPHEREx at 1-2.4 µm matches 2MASS, and after the drop W1
sits on the photosphere (K − W1 = 0.12); the fall in W1 in the figure is the excess leaving. I checked the step against the raw
single exposures, a constant control star in the same frames, both scan directions and the 7.7" neighbour, and could not make
it go away. The closest precedent I found is PDS 70, which lost its 3-5 µm inner-disk emission in NEOWISE for up to a year and
got it back (Gaidos et al. 2024). This one has been off at every look since (five NEOWISE visits and three SPHEREx seasons,
over 4.5 years), though a short return between looks can't be excluded. I can find no 10-24 µm measurement since WISE in 2010
(nothing in the JWST, SOFIA, Spitzer or ESO archives), so whether the disk is gone or only its inner rim is hidden is open.

**Question:** is this interesting enough to deserve a mid-infrared look now, and who works on disks like this?
